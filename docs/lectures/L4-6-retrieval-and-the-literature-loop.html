04

Retrieval-Augmented Generation: the Mechanism, and Where It Breaks

Taking the acronym apart — because "RAG" now names two quite different architectures, and choosing the wrong one is the most common expensive mistake in the field.

Why this lecture exists. The question that prompted it was a plain one: what is RAG, and which of its generic applications are relevant to this project? That question cannot be answered honestly in a paragraph, because the term has drifted. What was named in a 2020 paper as a specific trained architecture is now an umbrella covering everything from "paste three paragraphs into a prompt" to a multi-turn agent that reads a document, disagrees with it, and goes looking for a better one. Those two things share a technique and nothing else — not their cost, not their accuracy, not their failure modes, and not the engineering they require.

So this lecture builds the mechanism from the bottom. Lecture 05 then shows that this project has already specified a RAG system, in stricter terms than most production systems attempt, and never wrote the word down. Lecture 06 turns to the research loop — a second, quite different use of retrieval that is about to matter, because the next release is about mathematics and mathematics has a literature.

4.1 The three deficits RAG addresses — only one of which matters here

A language model's knowledge is frozen, undated, and unattributable. It was compressed out of a training corpus into weights, and the compression is lossy in a way that does not announce itself. The model cannot tell you where a fact came from, cannot tell you when it was true, and — the load-bearing problem — will produce a fact-shaped sentence in exactly the same confident register whether or not it has a fact behind it.

Retrieval fixes three distinct deficits, and they are worth separating because they have different costs:

  • Recency — the model does not know what happened after its cutoff. Solved trivially by putting recent text in the context.
  • Private knowledge — the model never saw your filings, your prior theses, your specs. Solved by indexing them.
  • ★ Attribution — the model cannot show its source. This is not solved by putting text in the context. Putting a document in the prompt makes a grounded answer possible; it does not make it happen, and it does not make it checkable. Almost all of the difficulty in this field lives here.

For this project, the first two are conveniences. The third is the whole point, and it is the one production systems handle worst.

A note on the term, so the paper does not confuse you

Lewis et al. (NeurIPS 2020) coined "retrieval-augmented generation" for a specific architecture: a dense retriever and a sequence-to-sequence generator, trained jointly end-to-end, with retrieval as a latent variable. That is not what the industry means today. Today "RAG" almost always means the untrained version — retrieve text, concatenate it into a prompt, generate. The modern practice is simpler and much less principled than the paper that named it. Read the paper knowing this, or you will spend an hour wondering where the gradient goes.

4.2 The four stages, and the failure that belongs to each

Every retrieval system, however dressed up, is these four stages. They fail independently, which is the single most useful fact about them: a system that produces a wrong answer has failed at exactly one of these, and treating it as one undifferentiated "the RAG isn't working" is how teams spend months tuning the stage that was already fine.

StageWhat happensThe diagnostic question
1 · IngestDocuments → parsed → split into retrievable unitsIf I read this chunk alone, with no neighbours, is the claim in it still true and still qualified?
2 · IndexUnits → a searchable structure: sparse keyword (BM25), dense vectors, or bothCan this index distinguish a statement from its negation? (A pure vector index largely cannot.)
★ 3 · RetrieveQuery → candidate units → reranked → top-k into the contextIs the passage that contains the answer actually in the k I returned? Measure this before looking at any generated output.
4 · GenerateModel answers conditioned on those units, ideally citing themIs every claim in the output traceable to a span in the context — and does the system refuse when it is not?

4.3 The uncomfortable fact: this is a search problem in a generation costume

The word "generation" in the name draws attention to the wrong half. In practice, retrieval quality dominates end-to-end quality, and by a wide margin. Upgrading the model is the intervention people reach for first and it is usually the smallest available improvement. Fixing retrieval is the boring one that works.

Three findings from the information-retrieval literature that save a great deal of time:

  • BM25 is a very strong baseline. Robertson and Zaragoza's probabilistic relevance framework is fifty years of accumulated tuning, it needs no training, no GPU and no embedding model, and the BEIR benchmark (Thakur et al., NeurIPS Datasets 2021) found that dense retrievers frequently fail to beat it when moved out of the domain they were trained on. Anyone who tells you to start with a vector database has skipped this result.
  • Hybrid plus reranking is the workhorse. Run BM25 and a dense retriever, fuse the candidate lists, then rerank the top ~100 with a cross-encoder — a model that reads the query and the passage together rather than embedding them separately. Nogueira and Cho (2019) is the reference point. This combination is close to the practical ceiling for most corpora and it is unglamorous.
  • ★ Embeddings encode topic, not truth value. "Momentum survives transaction costs" and "momentum does not survive transaction costs" are near-identical vectors. For a project whose entire purpose is telling a claim from its refutation, this is not a footnote — it is a reason to keep keyword and structural signals in the loop permanently.

The operational conclusion: build the search first, measure it in isolation, and only then attach a model. A retriever you have not measured is a random-number generator with good manners.

4.4 Two architectures, one word

This is the distinction that determines what you should build, and it maps neatly onto a concept Lecture 01 already established — binding time. In classic RAG the retrieval is bound once, before generation. In agentic retrieval it is bound repeatedly, during.

Classic / static RAGAgentic retrieval
ShapeOne query → top-k chunks → one prompt → one answerThe model searches, reads, judges, re-queries, follows a citation, opens a full document, decides it needs a different source

The drift, and why it matters to you

The field has moved hard from the first column to the second, for one mundane reason: context windows got large enough that "retrieve the whole document and read it" became affordable. Chunking was always a workaround for a constraint, not a good idea in its own right, and much of the elaborate chunking machinery of 2023 is now solving a problem that has partly gone away.

This bears directly on Lecture 06. The research loop you want is agentic retrieval over whole papers, not classic chunked RAG — and knowing that up front saves you from building the wrong thing well.

One caveat before you conclude that long context solves everything. Liu et al., Lost in the Middle (TACL 2024), showed that models attend unevenly across a long context — material at the beginning and end is used far more reliably than material in the middle. Stuffing fifty papers into a window is not equivalent to reading fifty papers. Ordering still matters, relevance filtering still matters, and Shi et al. (ICML 2023) showed that irrelevant context actively degrades reasoning rather than being harmlessly ignored. More context is not monotonically better.

4.5 The evaluation vocabulary — which you will need for F5

Retrieval evaluation has a mature vocabulary, and it decomposes failure along exactly the seam that matters: was the evidence there? versus did the model use it honestly? These are different bugs with different fixes, and a single end-to-end accuracy number conflates them.

MetricSideWhat it asks
Recall@kRetrievalOf the passages that could answer this, how many made the top k? ★ The ceiling on everything downstream.
Precision@k · nDCG · MRRRetrievalHow much of what I returned was junk, and how high did the good material rank?
Context precision / recallRetrievalThe same two questions phrased for a generation pipeline — the RAGAS framing (Es et al., EACL 2024).
★ Faithfulness / groundednessGenerationIs every claim in the answer entailed by the retrieved context? Not whether it is true — whether it is supported. A true-but-unsupported claim is still a defect.
★ Attribution (AIS)GenerationRashkin et al.'s Attributable to Identified Sources: would a reasonable person, reading the cited source, agree it says this? The formal version of claim→span binding.
Answer relevanceGenerationDid it answer the question actually asked, or an adjacent easier one?
Refusal rate on empty retrievalSystemWhen nothing relevant was found, how often did it say so instead of inventing? ⚠ Rarely measured. Directly the A7 rule.
The distinction to hold on to

Faithful ≠ correct. A perfectly faithful answer over a wrong document is confidently, traceably wrong — and that is still an improvement, because you can find it. An unfaithful answer over a right document is right by accident and will silently stop being right. Optimise for faithfulness first; it is the property that makes every other error findable.

4.6 What retrieval is not

  • Not a fix for reasoning. If the model cannot reason about the material, handing it more material produces a longer wrong answer.
  • ⛔ Not a fix for arithmetic. Retrieving a revenue figure and asking the model to compute a growth rate violates this project's standing rule — no arithmetic inside a prompt. Retrieval may deliver the number; deterministic code must consume it.
  • Not memory. Retrieval is stateless lookup. A system that "remembers" needs a write path, a decay policy and a conflict rule — genuinely harder, and a different design.
  • Not a substitute for a database. If the answer is a value in a table, query the table. Embedding structured data so a model can approximately find it is a well-populated category of wasted effort.

Exercises

  1. Take a filing you know well. Pick a claim whose meaning depends on a footnote three paragraphs away. Now chunk the document at 512 tokens and say which stage of §4.2 has already failed, before any model is involved.
  2. Construct two sentences about the same financial effect that are semantically opposite and would embed near-identically. Then state what signal, other than the embedding, would separate them — and whether that signal survives your chunking.
  3. You measure end-to-end accuracy at 60%. Design the two experiments that tell you whether the missing 40% is a retrieval failure or a generation failure. Which do you run first, and why is running only the end-to-end number a methodological error?
  4. Argue the case for BM25 over a vector index for a corpus of 2,000 finance papers. Then argue the reverse. Which argument required assumptions you cannot currently check?
  5. Define, as a testable statement, what "the system refused correctly" means. Then say how you would build a golden set for it — noting that it requires questions whose answer is deliberately absent from the corpus.

The RAG You Already Specified

The vision asks for claim→evidence-span binding and measured grounding rates. That is a retrieval-augmented generation contract, written more strictly than most shipped systems attempt — and the word appears nowhere in it.

The natural next question after Lecture 04 is "which of these generic applications are relevant to us?" — and the honest answer is sharper than a catalogue. Two of this project's existing capabilities are a retrieval system, described in the vocabulary of explainability rather than the vocabulary of retrieval. Recognising that is worth more than any list, because it means the RAG-evaluation literature can be read as if it were written about F4 and F5.

5.2 C3 is a faithfulness contract with a stricter bar than the field's

Vision §4 states capability C3 as: "attribution for the ranker, claim→evidence-span binding for the LLM, counterfactuals, calibrated confidence." And C6 lists among the evaluation harness's obligations: "golden sets, citation and grounding rates, calibration, faithfulness tests."

Read those two clauses next to §4.5 of the previous lecture and the correspondence is exact. Claim→span binding is the attribution problem — Rashkin et al.'s AIS framework, and the mechanism Menick et al. built in GopherCite, where a model is trained to support each assertion with a verbatim quoted span. Grounding rate is faithfulness, measured.

And the bar set here is higher than the industry norm in a specific, expensive way:

Granularity of citationWho does thisWhat it can actually verify
Document-level — "source: 2024 10-K"Most production RAGAlmost nothing. The document is 200 pages; the claim may appear nowhere in it.
Chunk-level — "source: chunk 47"Better systemsNarrows it to a paragraph. A reviewer can check, slowly.
★ Span-level — this sentence ↔ these charactersWhat C3 demandsMechanical verification. A test can assert it. This is what makes F5's "grounding rate" a computable number rather than a human review exercise.
The claim of this lecture, in one line

F9 is the retrieval half and F4 is the grounded-generation half of a RAG system; F5 is its retrieval-and-attribution evaluation harness. None of the three uses the word. That is fine — but it means the RAG literature is directly applicable to specs already written, and that the metrics in §4.5 are candidate acceptance criteria rather than background reading.

5.3 Three standing rules become three design constraints

The constitution and the vision already contain rules that, translated into retrieval terms, decide most of the architecture. They were not written with retrieval in mind, which is a good sign — it suggests they are principles rather than preferences.

Existing ruleBecomes, in a retrieval system
A7 — fail loud, never silent. A degraded input raises; it never emits a plausible-looking default★ Empty or low-confidence retrieval must produce a refusal, not a synthesis. This is the single most frequently violated rule in production RAG, because a refusal looks like a broken product and a fluent guess looks like a working one. It needs to be an acceptance criterion with a golden set of deliberately unanswerable questions, or it will erode.
No arithmetic inside a prompt (§7 non-goal)Retrieval may deliver text and retrieved figures. A retrieved figure is an input to deterministic code, never an operand the model multiplies. The boundary is enforced by where the number goes next, not by prompt wording.
C7 trace & replay · C8 provenanceEvery retrieval is traced: the query as issued, the index version, the returned document ids, their scores, the reranker version, and the k. Without these an explanation cannot be replayed, because the same question against a re-indexed corpus is a different question. ⚠ This is the vision's own C6/C7 argument again — cheap to record now, impossible to reconstruct from history that never recorded it.
A5 — the control plane stays deterministicChoosing whether to retrieve, and from which source, is a routing decision: a pure function with a recorded reason. Judging which retrieved passage supports the claim is analysis, and belongs in a seat with a contract — not in the orchestrator.

5.4 Where retrieval sits in the seat model — and the firewall trap

This section exists because there is a design that looks obviously sensible and quietly breaches a structural fence.

Vision §3.2 states the analyst firewall: "Seats 2 and 3 reach their views independently. Neither consumes the other's conclusion; both freely consume the same data and the same computed numbers from seat 8." A shared evidence index is data — both analytical seats may query it, and should. That is not the trap.

★ The trap is corpus growth. The natural, lazy instinct when building an index is to index everything the system has ever produced — including prior theses, prior verdicts, prior explanation pages. The moment seat 3 retrieves a passage from seat 2's published thesis, the firewall has been breached by a similarity score, with no import statement to catch it and nothing in ownership.yaml to deny it. The fence is enforced at the corpus boundary, not at the query.

The consequence is a rule worth writing into F9 when it is drafted: an evidence index consumed by an analytical seat contains external evidence and seat-8 computed numbers only. Seat outputs are indexed in a separate corpus that only seat 6 may query.

Two further placements follow directly from the seat rules:

  • Seat 6 / D-13 does not retrieve. Vision §4.2 is explicit: it consumes published outputs and never sees in-progress analysis. Its inputs are the spans each seat published on its own output contract. If D-13 ever needs to go and fetch supporting evidence itself, the attribution obligation upstream was under-specified — fix it there.
  • Seat 7 may route to a retriever, never judge its results. "The orchestrator carries, never computes" applies unchanged. Deciding to search is control flow. Deciding what the search means is analysis.

Exercises

  1. Write claim→span binding as a testable acceptance criterion in the form AC-4.k. It must be checkable by a test with no human in the loop. State what the test does when the model emits a claim with no span.
  2. Design the golden set for the refusal rule in §5.3. It requires questions whose answers are deliberately absent. Say how you construct such questions without accidentally making them trivially recognisable as traps.
  3. An analyst asks for "the same retrieval index for both seat 2 and seat 3, it's cheaper." Give the case for, the case against, and the precise line in vision §3.2 that decides it. Then write the corpus-boundary rule as one sentence for F9.
  4. You re-index the corpus with a better embedding model. Every stored explanation now cites document ids that rank differently. What must have been traced for the old explanations to remain replayable — and if it was not, what have you lost?
  5. Argue whether "grounding rate" should gate a release or merely accrue. Use the F7a/F7b distinction as your model, and say which one it resembles.
06

The Literature Loop: Making the Next Release's Mathematics Defensible

The next release is quantitative method, risk, collective decision, the academics of fundamental analysis, and orchestrator design. All of those have a literature. This lecture is about how to actually reach it — and why the rule matters more than the tool.

The brief, restated. The next release turns from platform scaffolding to substance: quantitative method, risk, collective decision-making, the academic basis of fundamental analysis, and the design of the orchestrator. For that release the assistant must be forced through real academic research from reputable sources, rather than allowed to produce a confident summary from memory. The fund manager holds a University of Ottawa graduate library entitlement, which is a genuine asset. The questions that follow are: what tooling exists, what does the assistant already support, and if nothing fits, is building something feasible?

The answers are, briefly: quite a lot exists and most of it is free; the assistant can already do the open-access half today with no new tooling at all; and building the rest is feasible but should be deferred until a specific search has failed without it. The rest of this lecture is why.

6.1 The failure this is meant to prevent — name it precisely

Ask a language model to design a risk module and it will produce fluent, plausible, textbook-shaped output: Kelly sizing, CVaR, hierarchical risk parity, ensemble disagreement as an uncertainty proxy, a Bayesian shrinkage estimator for the covariance matrix. Every item will be a real method with a real name. The output will read like a competent literature review.

What it will be missing is everything that makes a literature review worth reading:

  • the regime the original result was established in — which market, which period, which universe, which turnover assumption
  • the qualifications the authors themselves attached, which are usually in section 5 and never in the abstract
  • the failed replications, and the sizeable literature showing that a great many published results shrink or vanish out of sample
  • the fact that a later paper demolished the result that everyone still cites, and the citation kept propagating anyway

That output is memory, not scholarship. It has no provenance, it cannot be audited, and — the part that matters for this project specifically — it is indistinguishable in tone from the version that has all four.

Why this is the same defect the project already refuses

The vision closes on the property it calls the most valuable thing this project has: the willingness to refuse its own model. The record shows a walk-forward IC of 0.0203 at t = 1.42 and declines promotion, noting "a signal that passed would not have been shown to work."

An uncited method claim in a design document is the design-tier version of that unvalidated signal. It looks like a result. It has not been shown to be one. Applying the same instinct one tier earlier — at the point where a method is chosen rather than where it is measured — is what this whole lecture is arguing for.

6.2 The rule does more work than the tool

There are three interventions available, and they are not equally powerful. In descending order of effect per unit of effort:

  1. ★ A rule that makes an uncited method claim a process violation rather than a stylistic preference. Free. Available today. Does most of the work.
  2. Access to the actual papers, so the citations are real and the method section has been read rather than the abstract. This is where tooling and the library entitlement enter.
  3. A durable corpus, so release 3 does not re-litigate what release 2 settled, and so a decision can point at its justification months later.

⚠ The ordering is the point. Building (2) and (3) while skipping (1) produces a good search tool and no obligation to use it. The rule is what converts "go and read the literature" from an aspiration into a gate — and gates are this project's established mechanism.

A concrete draft, sized to drop into the design-tier template:

Proposed rule — for sdd/design/_templates/

A design proposal that adopts a quantitative method must cite the primary source by DOI or stable identifier; state the data regime in which the result was established (market, period, universe, and any turnover or cost assumption); and name at least one paper that limits, disputes, or fails to replicate it.

★ Where no dissenting work is located, the proposal records "no dissent found" — never "undisputed."

That last clause is doing real work. Absence of located evidence is not evidence of absence, and the difference between the two phrasings is the difference between a search that was performed and a conclusion that was assumed. It also creates an honest audit trail: a later reader can see that a search happened and came up empty, which is information.

Where the citations live is already decided. This directory's own convention holds: citations belong in docs/lectures/ and in sdd/design/, not in specs. A spec states what must be true; it does not argue from literature. So the literature loop feeds the design and study tiers, and the spec that graduates out of them records the decision and its rationale, not the bibliography.

6.4 The access policy — two modes, and why every database is open to us

The Library's reply (2026-09-09) answered the access question and, in the process, dissolved a confusion that this section previously carried. The licence clauses the librarian quoted are real and specific — but they do not all attach to the same activity. Separating the two modes is what makes the whole catalogue usable without ambiguity.

Mode A — readingMode B — mining

1 · Mode A is the default and it is unrestricted. Elsevier, Wiley, OUP, Taylor & Francis, Springer, JSTOR, Scopus, WRDS and every other licensed resource are read, downloaded and kept in the reference manager as ordinary graduate-student use. No tooling, no token and no special justification is required, and nothing in this lecture should be read as discouraging it.

2 · Mode B is entered per publisher, on their terms. Accept the agreement, hold the token in the environment (never the repo), acknowledge the source in anything written up, do not redistribute, and treat harvested full text as working storage — see §6.6.

3 · Prefer Mode A. Not for caution — because §6.7 is right: the goal is precision over roughly thirty papers read properly, and thirty papers is a Mode A problem. Mode B earns its token when a question genuinely requires scale.

The project's standing, recorded rather than assumed

Elsevier's and OUP's grants are for non-commercial research and scholarly or educational use. That is a condition on the licence, so the project's position on it is worth writing down once, plainly, rather than leaving it to be re-derived under pressure:

  • This is a personal project and the research is self-education. The literature work described in these lectures exists so the fund manager understands the mathematics he is implementing — quantitative method, risk, evaluation design. That is scholarly and educational use in the ordinary sense of both words.
  • Nothing is sold, licensed or operated for anyone else. No third-party capital, no clients, no product, no service. Vision §1.1 purpose 2 is explicitly a learning and portfolio objective; vision §7 permanently forbids automatic execution and any path from a report to a broker order.
  • The non-compete clause is satisfied on its face. Elsevier's condition is that the analysis must not compete with, substitute for or replicate publisher products — abstracting databases, indexes, full-text services. A private investment-method study produces none of those and could not substitute for one.
⚠ Review trigger — the one condition that would change this

The paragraph above is a statement about today, 2026-09-09, and it is accurate today. If the system is ever operated on a third party's capital, sold, licensed, or turned into a service for anyone other than the fund manager, the non-commercial basis for Mode B access must be re-examined before any further Mode B retrieval — and the Library asked again.

Recording the trigger costs nothing now and is what keeps the position honest rather than convenient. It is the same instinct as the rest of this repository: state the assumption, date it, and say what would falsify it. Mode A is unaffected by any of this — a graduate student reading licensed articles remains a graduate student reading licensed articles.

What stays out of bounds, regardless

One thing does not become acceptable under any framing, and it is worth keeping visible because it is the one that would actually cost something:

  • ⛔ Scripting an EZproxy session to bulk-download PDFs. At individual scale it gets the account suspended, ending the research loop. At any noticeable scale, publishers respond by blocking the institution's whole IP range — a cost borne by several thousand people who did nothing. The sanctioned API route now exists for all four publishers, so there is no longer any reason to reach for the unsanctioned one.

The Library also noted that publishers do not publish standard volume limits and do not disclose them to the institution. If a limit is not stated when access is granted, ask that publisher directly. ★ Asking is cheap; discovering the limit by tripping it is not.

The division of labour that works — and stays inside every licence

For open-access literature, no new tool is required at all. The assistant can query OpenAlex, walk the citation graph in both directions, pull citation contexts from Semantic Scholar, fetch arXiv PDFs and read them — today, with curl and Read.

For paywalled full text the loop is: assistant searches, ranks and hands over a DOI shortlist → the human fetches through the library in a browser → the PDFs land in a folder → the assistant reads them. The human is the authenticated agent; the assistant is the search-and-synthesis layer. ★ This is not a workaround for a missing feature. It is the correct boundary, and it happens to be the one that keeps the institution's access intact.

On third-party MCP servers. Community MCP servers exist for arXiv, Semantic Scholar, PubMed and Zotero, and would make these tools native in any session. Quality varies considerably and I would not vouch for any specific one without reading it. In a repository with this governance posture — a PreToolUse gate, an ownership map, a rule against silent failure — installing an unread third-party server that can make network calls deserves the same scrutiny as any other dependency. It is also not obviously better than fifty lines of curl you wrote yourself.

TierWhat it isEffortWhat it buys

★ The Tier 2 storage split

The TDM clauses require that mined copies be deleted once the computational analysis is done. That constrains one shelf of the corpus, not the corpus:

Durable, indefinitely: metadata and DOIs · your own notes and extracts · the citation graph · search results and their provenance · open-access full text · ★ anything obtained in Mode A and kept in the reference manager, which is ordinary personal retention.

Working storage, cleared after the analysis: full text harvested through a publisher TDM API in Mode B.

So the durable corpus survives intact — the deletion rule bites on bulk-mined text, which is the shelf you were least likely to need twice anyway. Note Elsevier’s additional condition that mined copies be stored on a secure server in Canada: on a single-machine project in Ottawa that is satisfied by construction, but it is a reason never to sync that particular directory to an offshore cloud drive.

The stop rule

Build tier n+1 only when you can name a specific search that tier n could not perform. Not "it would be nicer" — a search you actually wanted and could not run. This is the same discipline as A4, abstraction is earned, applied to tooling: a tier exists when a second real need for it has appeared, not speculatively.

6.7 Why you probably should not chunk, and probably do not need vectors

This is the most contrarian section of the lecture and the one most likely to save a wasted week.

First: for a corpus of a few thousand papers, you almost certainly do not need embeddings. SQLite FTS5 gives you BM25 over full text in the standard library, ripgrep handles everything it misses, and — per §4.3 — BM25 is a strong baseline that dense retrieval frequently fails to beat out of domain. This costs an afternoon rather than a week and has no model to maintain, no dimension to choose, and no re-indexing bill when a better embedding model appears.

★★ Second, and more important: chunked RAG is the wrong architecture for methods papers. The thing you are actually trying to retrieve from a quantitative paper is the method — and the method does not live in a paragraph. It lives in the equations, the table of specifications, the description of the estimation window, the robustness section, and very often the appendix. A 512-token chunk severs an estimator from its assumptions and hands you a fragment that is worse than useless, because it reads as complete.

What you want is find the paper, then read the whole paper — agentic retrieval per §4.4, not classic RAG. The goal is precision over roughly thirty papers, not recall over 250 million.

★ The best retriever in this domain is not a retriever

It is the citation graph. Starting from one seed paper you trust and walking cited-by forward and references backward gives higher precision than any embedding search over the same corpus — because every edge in that graph was placed there by a human being who read both papers. OpenAlex and Semantic Scholar both expose it in both directions, free.

It also answers the question §6.2's rule demands and that similarity search is structurally bad at: who disagreed with this? Semantic Scholar's citation contexts take it further — the sentence in which a later paper cites an earlier one frequently contains the disagreement verbatim. That is a dissent-finding mechanism, not just a discovery one.

Two corroborations from the Library worth acting on. Asked what alerting to use, the librarian recommended Scopus alerts driven by a deliberately narrow query rather than a whole subject area — and explicitly advised against Google Scholar alerts, for exactly the reason this lecture keeps returning to: an over-broad query inundates you, and precision beats recall when a human has to read the results.

★ The second is Research Rabbit, recommended for finding related work through both keywords and citation networks. That is §6.7's argument arriving independently from a professional librarian: the citation graph is the high-precision discovery mechanism in this domain, and the tools that expose it are the ones worth learning. Try it before building Tier 1 — it may close the gap on its own.

6.8 Where such a tool lives — and why not in src/

⚠ Not in src/. Three independent reasons, any one sufficient:

  • PG-8: this repository's tree must remain a superset of the pipeline's src/** so that absorption is a merge and not a restructuring. A literature tool under src/ pollutes that merge for zero benefit.
  • It has no seat and no domain. It produces nothing anyone would trade on, so it sits outside the authority model entirely — which is precisely why it needs no governance.
  • Vision §7 lists as a durable non-goal: "not a general-purpose research tool." That non-goal is about the product. A dev-time tool does not violate it — but putting it in src/ would make it look like it does, to a future reader who has only the tree.

So: tools/litsearch/, or a .claude/skills/ skill wrapping it — the same category as fmp-api, which is already exactly this: hand-built, dev-time, authoritative about an external system, governing nothing. Its outputs land in docs/lectures/ and sdd/design/.

The drift to guard against

Do not let the dev-time research tool grow into the product's retriever by accident. They will look similar and share code, and that is exactly how an ungoverned component ends up on the path to a number someone trades on.

If retrieval enters the product it enters under F9 / F4, with acceptance criteria, a trace, a corpus boundary (§5.4) and F5 measurement. The research tool has none of those and needs none — because nothing it produces is ever consumed by anything but a human writing a design document.

6.9 So — is the research intention the same thing as RAG?

The closing question of the discussion that produced these notes. The answer: yes, they are siblings — and treating them as the same system would be a mistake. They share a technique and almost nothing else.

The non-obvious payoff

The research loop is a low-stakes rehearsal for the high-stakes one. Building it teaches, at first hand, what retrieval evaluation actually feels like: that top-k is where accuracy dies; that span-level grounding is far harder than document-level; that a model will confidently synthesise across two papers that flatly contradict each other and produce a fluent sentence containing neither position.

You learn all of that where the only casualty is a mediocre design document. Then you build F4's claim→span binding, already burned by the easy version.

The one-sentence version

Write the rule that makes an uncited method claim a violation; get sanctioned library access through the library; search the citation graph rather than an embedding index; read whole papers rather than chunks; and keep the whole thing out of src/.

Exercises

  1. Take one method you already intend to use in the next release. Apply the §6.2 rule to it by hand: primary source, regime, and one dissenting paper. Time yourself. That number is the real cost of the rule, and it is the number that decides whether the rule survives contact with a deadline.
  2. Pick a seed paper and walk cited-by forward two hops in OpenAlex. Then run a keyword search for the same topic. Compare the two result sets and say which found the dissent — and why.
  3. Write the stop-rule test for Tier 1 as a concrete sentence: what search, exactly, would Tier 0 fail at? If you cannot write it, that is the answer.
  4. The rule in §6.2 requires naming a paper that disputes the method. Argue the case that this requirement is too strong for a well-established method — then repair the rule so it survives your own objection without becoming unenforceable.
  5. Sketch the trace record a Tier 1 search would emit for C7-style replay: what fields must it carry for a search run today to be reproducible in eighteen months, given that OpenAlex is a living index? Note which fields are impossible and say what follows.
  6. You find a 2011 paper establishing a result, and a 2019 paper failing to replicate it, and a 2023 paper defending the original with a different dataset. Under the §6.2 rule, what does the design document record — and what does the spec that graduates from it record?
