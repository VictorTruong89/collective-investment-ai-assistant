PART 1

Why the model exists, and where it sits

A machine-learning model is never "the system". It is one seat in a fund with several, and its job is narrow on purpose.

1.1The fund this model works in

The Collective Investment AI Assistant is organised like a small professional fund. Each seat asks one question and is fenced from the others, so that the person who has an idea is not also the person who checks it or the person who decides.

SeatWhoQuestion it answersAuthority
1 · PM & Traderthe human fund managerDo I buy, sell, or do nothing?the only decision
2 · Fundamental AnalystAI (LLM)Is this company's story sound? What would prove it wrong?recommend
3 · Quantitative AnalystAI (this model + the quant gates)Out of ~500 stocks, which look most attractive for the next month?recommend
4 · RiskAIWhat could go wrong with this book?alert only
5 · Portfolio StrategyAIHow big could a position be?arithmetic only
6 · Decision MediatorAIWhat do the seats say together, and why?composes, originates nothing

The selection model is seat 3's engine. Its whole contract is one sentence: given what was knowable at this morning's open, order the S&P 500 from most to least attractive over the next 21 trading days. It does not size positions, does not decide, and does not explain the business — those are other seats' jobs.

1.2Three deliberate limits on its role

  1. It nominates, it never decides. A high rank is an invitation to look, not an instruction to buy. Only seat 1, a human, decides.
  2. It works at one horizon only — one month (21 trading days). The code refuses to train a selection model at any other horizon. A one-week horizon is a timing question (it may delay or veto, never nominate), and a quarter is research only. Mixing them was a real defect in the older pipeline this repo absorbed: a model trained to predict next week was being used to choose stocks for next month.
  3. It must earn its place. A trained model is held for review and can only be promoted if every gate in Part 5 passes. Until then the card is ranked by a transparent rule — 21-day momentum scaled by volatility — and the card states which ranked it.
Real-life analogy — the scout and the coach

A football club's scout watches hundreds of young players and hands the coach a shortlist. The scout does not pick the team, does not negotiate contracts, and is judged on one thing: did the players on my list turn out better than the ones I left off? That is exactly how this model is judged — not by whether its favourite stock went up, but by whether its ordering was right on average.

1.3Why use machine learning at all?

Finance research has documented many weak signals that each slightly predict which stocks do better: recent winners keep winning for a while (momentum), cheap stocks beat expensive ones over long periods (value), calmer stocks do better than their risk suggests (low volatility), profitable firms beat unprofitable ones (quality). Each one alone is faint, and they interact — momentum behaves differently in small and large firms; a high margin means something different for a software firm than a grocer.

A model's job is to combine many faint signals, including their interactions, in a way that a hand-written formula would not. Gu, Kelly and Xiu (2020) showed on US data that tree ensembles and neural networks combine such signals better than linear regression, mainly because they capture non-linearities and interactions. That is the hypothesis this model tests. It is a hypothesis, not a promise — Parts 6 and 7 show how the test came out.

Check yourself
  1. Why is a one-week model not allowed to nominate stocks for a one-month holding?
  2. If the model is refused, what ranks the daily card?
Answers

(1) It was trained to answer a different question (short-term timing), so its errors are tuned to the wrong target. (2) A transparent momentum-over-volatility rule, and the card says "ranked by the rule".

PART 2

The financial-engineering problem

Predicting a stock's return is nearly hopeless. Predicting which of two stocks will do better is merely very hard. The model is built for the second.

2.1Cross-sectional, not time-series

A time-series question asks "will Apple go up next month?". A cross-sectional question asks "next month, will Apple do better or worse than the other 500 stocks?". The second is easier because a large part of every stock's move is the market moving all of them together, and a ranking ignores that common part.

ri,t = βi·rmarket,t + (sector effect) + εi,t A stock's return is part market, part sector, part its own story (ε). Ranking stocks on the same day cancels most of the market term — every stock shares it. The model's edge, if any, lives in the ε part.
Real-life example — the marathon

Predicting a runner's finishing time depends heavily on the weather on race day, which you cannot know. Predicting the finishing order does not: a hot day slows everyone. Ranking stocks is predicting the finishing order.

2.2What exactly is predicted — the label

For every stock i on every trading day t, the training target ("label") is:

yi,t = openi(t + 21) ÷ openi(t) − 1 The return from this morning's open to the open 21 trading days later, split- and dividend-adjusted. Open-to-open, both ends, because the system decides at the open: a label measured from the close would credit the model with a move that happened before anyone could act on it.

The model is fitted to predict y, but only the order of its predictions is ever used. A prediction of +3% versus +1% means "rank this one higher", not "expect 3%".

2.3How good is "good"? The information coefficient

The standard score for a ranking signal is the information coefficient (IC): on each day, the rank correlation (Spearman) between the model's scores and the returns that actually followed, then averaged over days.

ICt = Spearman( score·,t , y·,t ) · IC = meant(ICt) +1 means a perfect ordering, 0 means none, −1 a perfectly reversed one.

Students are usually shocked by how small good ICs are. In equity selection, a stable IC of 0.02–0.05 is respectable (Grinold & Kahn, 2000). The reason is that monthly stock returns are mostly noise: the predictable part is a few per cent of the variation.

Real-life example — the casino

A roulette wheel with a single zero gives the house an edge of about 2.7%. On any single spin the house loses almost half the time. Over a million spins it is a business. An IC of 0.03 is the same kind of thing: useless for one stock in one month, valuable only if applied to many stocks over many months — and only if it is real. Part 5 is about checking that it is real.

Check yourself
  1. Why does a ranking model not need to forecast the market?
  2. A signal has IC = 0.03. Roughly how often does it rank a random pair of stocks correctly?
Answers

(1) The market term is shared by every stock on a date, so it cancels out of the ordering. (2) Only slightly better than a coin: for small correlations, the chance of ordering a pair correctly is about ½ + IC/π ≈ 51%. Edges in this business are tiny.

3.3The rule that matters most: point-in-time

Every feature must be knowable on the morning it is used. A company's March-quarter results are not knowable on 31 March; they become knowable when the 10-Q is filed, typically six weeks later. So fundamentals are joined on their filing date, looking backward only. Joining on the quarter's end date would hand the model six weeks of hindsight on every row — and produce a beautiful, worthless backtest.

A real defect, found and fixed the day this model was trained (DX-159)

A company's annual report repeats the previous two years' numbers as comparatives. The fact store keeps every copy, each stamped with its own filing date — ADM's 2022 net income appears four times: in the 10-K of February 2023, the 10-K of March 2024, an amended 10-K/A of 18 November 2024, and the 10-K of February 2025.

Training used to read the store once, as of today, so each period carried its latest filing date. Joined backward onto ten years of dates, that meant on 3 June 2024 the model saw ADM's 2020 figures. Measured on 24 stocks: only 13% of historical rows saw the right quarter; the median row was 371 days stale. No look-ahead — but a year-old picture of every company.

The fix replays the store at every filing date: "what did we know on the day this filing landed?" — and computes the features from that. After the fix, 798 of 798 checked rows matched a true point-in-time read. A bonus: ADM's restatement is now handled honestly — the original figures before 18 Nov 2024, the amended ones after.

Real-life example — the newspaper archive

To test a stock-picking rule from 2019, you must read the 2019 newspaper, not today's Wikipedia page about 2019. Wikipedia has been corrected and updated since; the paper has not. Point-in-time data is the newspaper archive. The DX-159 bug was like reading the 2019 paper — but the 2017 edition of it.

3.4Two honest caveats about the inputs

  • Missing is not zero. A bank has no "gross margin"; a retailer reports no R&D. Those cells are left empty (null), never filled with 0 — a zero margin and an unknown margin are different facts. Mean coverage across the 61 features was 75%; f_rd_to_revenue is under 25% filled.
  • Finding while writing this lecture: the training run supplies no sector map, so the five *_sector_z features are computed with every stock in one "unknown" group — they are market-wide z-scores, not sector-relative ones. The code's own documentation says they should be null in that case. The model still uses them heavily (Figure 5), but they do not mean what their names say. Recorded as DX-160 and fixed later the same day: training and the daily run now read the GICS map in config/sp500_tickers.yaml, and an unknown sector gives null. The case-study model below was fitted before the fix.

PART 4

The architecture — gradient-boosted decision trees (LightGBM)

Many small, shallow trees, each correcting the mistakes of the ones before it.

4.1One decision tree

A decision tree is a flowchart of yes/no questions about the features, ending in a prediction. The algorithm chooses each question to split the data into two groups whose returns are as different as possible.

Real-life analogy — twenty questions

A tree plays "twenty questions" with a stock: Is it calm? Is it more profitable than average? Is it above its 50-day average? Each answer narrows the group, and the final group's average past return is the guess.

4.2Boosting: many weak trees, each fixing the last

One tree is crude and unstable. Gradient boosting (Friedman, 2001) builds trees one after another. Tree 1 makes a rough guess. Tree 2 is fitted to what tree 1 got wrong (the residuals). Tree 3 to what trees 1+2 got wrong, and so on. The final prediction is the sum, with each tree's contribution shrunk by a learning rate so no single tree dominates.

Fm(x) = Fm−1(x) + η · hm(x), hm fitted to y − Fm−1(x) F is the running prediction, hm the m-th small tree, η the learning rate (0.03 here). For squared error, "fit the gradient" is literally "fit the residuals".
Real-life analogy — putting in golf

The first putt gets you near the hole. Each next putt corrects the remaining distance. A small learning rate is a cautious golfer who never tries to sink a long putt in one go — slower, but much less likely to overshoot.

Caveat — the name says "rank", the objective says "regression"

The family is called gbdt.lightgbm_rank.v1, but the estimator is LGBMRegressor fitted on raw 21-day returns. So the trees spend effort explaining things a ranking does not care about — above all, the market's month-to-month swings, which are shared by every stock. Only the order of predictions is used afterwards.

Update, 8 Oct: since fit 3 the trees are fitted on each day's return rank as a normal score (--target xs_gauss, now the default) — the single largest improvement in case study II (§7.3). Part 8 lists further options, including a true ranking objective.

4.4Why gradient-boosted trees, and not something else?

vs. linear regression

Linear models cannot see interactions or thresholds unless you hand-craft them, and they are thrown off by outliers and skewed ratios. Trees split on order, so a ratio of 10 or 10,000 lands in the same branch. Gu, Kelly & Xiu (2020) report trees and neural nets clearly beating linear models on US stock returns.

vs. neural networks

On tabular data — rows of mixed, unscaled, partly missing columns — tree ensembles still usually match or beat deep networks (Grinsztajn, Oyallon & Varoquaux, 2022), and stock data has far lower signal-to-noise than those benchmarks. The project keeps neural models in the research seat until one beats the trees on the same protocol.

Handles missing values

LightGBM learns which branch a missing value should go down. A bank with no gross margin is routed sensibly, without inventing a zero.

Fast and reproducible

LightGBM (Ke et al., 2017) fits 1.2 million rows × 61 features in about 25 seconds here, with deterministic settings — which matters when the same fit must be re-run five times inside cross-validation.

PART 5

How the model is trained — and how it avoids fooling itself

Fitting is the easy 25 seconds. Almost all of the engineering is about measuring the fit honestly.

5.1The training run, step by step

  1. Fundamentals — download each company's XBRL facts from SEC EDGAR into an append-only store (5 min for 510 names).
  2. Prices — ten years of daily OHLCV from FMP for the whole S&P 500 (11½ min).
  3. Calendar check — scan the window for splits, delistings, restatements and other events that can corrupt a price series; halt on anything unhandled.
  4. Features panel — the 61 features for every (stock, day): 1,248,338 rows (4 min, mostly the point-in-time replay).
  5. Labels — the 21-day open-to-open return for each row.
  6. Fit — one LightGBM model on all of it (the production candidate).
  7. Validation — five more fits, each tested on a period it never saw (§5.2) → the out-of-sample IC.
  8. Backtest & gates — simulate holding the top 10, measure breadth, apply the three gates (§5.4).
  9. Hold for review — the model is written to disk; promotion needs a human command and still cannot override a failed gate.

5.2Validation without leakage: purged, embargoed folds

The first rule of machine learning is "test on data the model has not seen". In finance this is harder than it looks, because labels overlap in time. The label for 2 January is the return through ~1 February; the label for 3 January covers nearly the same days. If 2 January is in training and 3 January in testing, the model has effectively seen the test answer.

Real-life analogy — the leaked exam

If this year's exam re-uses 20 of last year's 21 questions, a student who memorised last year's paper "scores" brilliantly without learning anything. Purging means removing from the study pack every past paper that shares questions with the exam.

The fix (López de Prado, 2018) is to cut the timeline into five blocks. Each block takes a turn as the test set; training uses everything else except a purge gap on both sides of the test block (no training label may reach into it) and an embargo after it (5 days, for serial correlation).

Two details worth knowing
  • The purge length is the longest horizon in the set (63, the quarterly research horizon), not the 21 being trained — so adding a longer horizon later can never silently under-purge.
  • The purge is applied as 63 calendar days (≈ 43 trading days). That is more than the 21-trading-day label needs, so this fit is safe; but the horizon is defined in trading days, and for a true 63-trading-day label (~91 calendar days) the gap would be too short. Noted while writing this lecture.

5.3Is the IC real? Standard errors that respect time

An average IC is meaningless without its uncertainty. The naive t-statistic treats each of the 2,490 daily ICs as independent. They are not: with a 21-day label, consecutive days share 20 of their 21 days of returns. Two corrections are reported:

  • Null SE (the old way): divide by √(dates ÷ 21) — "about 119 independent months". Reported as the minimum detectable effect, not used to decide.
  • Realised SE (decisive): the Newey–West estimator (Newey & West, 1987), which measures how strongly the daily ICs actually move together and widens the error bar accordingly, using 20 lags.
t = mean IC ÷ SE · pass if |mean IC| ≥ 3 × SENewey–West Why 3 and not the textbook 2? Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) count hundreds of published "factors" and show that, after so much searching, a new one needs t > 3 to be credible.
Real-life example — the coin-flipping contest

Ask 1,000 people to flip a coin ten times. About one will get ten heads by pure luck, and will look like a genius. If the whole industry tests thousands of signals, some will look brilliant by chance. A higher bar (t ≥ 3) is how you avoid hiring the lucky flipper.

5.4The three gates

GateQuestionPasses when
ic_significanceIs the out-of-sample IC distinguishable from zero?|IC| ≥ 3 × Newey–West SE
breadthIs the signal applied to enough independent bets to matter?implied IR = |IC| · √BR ≥ 0.5 (provisional)
cost_survivalDoes the strategy still make money after trading costs?net return > 0

The breadth gate comes from the Fundamental Law of Active Management (Grinold, 1989):

IR ≈ IC × √BR IR is the information ratio (excess return ÷ its volatility — a Sharpe ratio against a benchmark). BR is breadth: the number of independent bets per year. A tiny IC can produce a decent IR — but only with many truly independent bets.

6.2The five folds — a fading signal

Three explanations fit this shape, and a good analyst keeps all three open until tested:

  1. Signal decay. Published anomalies weaken after publication as investors trade on them — McLean & Pontiff (2016) find anomaly returns about 26% lower just after the sample a paper studied, and about 58% lower once it is published.
  2. Regime. 2016–18 was a calm, trending market where momentum and low-volatility signals worked well; 2020–22 had a pandemic crash, a meme-stock episode and a rate shock.
  3. The fold design. Fold 0 was trained on 2018–26 and tested on 2016–18. A model that learned "what worked later" may look prescient about the earlier period. A true walk-forward test would separate this from decay — now built, and §7.4 gives its answer.

Real-life example — the streaky shooter

A basketball player hits 55% of shots. If her shots are independent, 200 attempts prove she is better than 50%. If she runs hot and cold for weeks at a time, those 200 shots are really a few dozen "streaks", and the evidence is much weaker. Newey–West measures how streaky the daily IC is.

6.4What the model leans on — feature importance

The picture reads like a summary of the factor literature: volatility (low-vol anomaly), profitability (quality), size, value (market-to-book, Graham number), and trend (50-day). That is reassuring — the model rediscovered known factors rather than something exotic. It is also a warning: known factors are the ones most likely to have decayed (§6.2).

Read importance with care
  • Gain importance says what the trees used, measured in-sample. It does not say what predicts out of sample, and it splits credit arbitrarily between correlated features (vol_20d and rank_vol_20d are nearly the same thing).
  • f_rd_to_revenue is under 25% filled yet ranks 6th. Part of its value may be whether it is missing — firms that report R&D are mostly tech and pharma — so it may be acting as a sector label.
  • f_net_margin_sector_z is not actually sector-relative in this run (§3.4).

Why not believe 34% a year? Three reasons, in order of size:

  1. Survivorship bias. The universe is today's S&P 500 applied back to 2016. Every stock in it survived and grew enough to be in the index in 2026. Companies that collapsed, were acquired cheaply or dropped out are missing — exactly the ones a backtest would have held and lost money on (Brown, Goetzmann, Ibbotson & Ross, 1992). The project cannot fix this on its current data plan and stamps every backtest "BIASED".
  2. The numbers disagree with each other. The fundamental law with IC 0.0227 predicts an information ratio near 0.15. A 20%-a-year excess return with a top-10 portfolio's typical tracking error of ~15–25% would imply an IR near 1. The gap is too big to be skill; it is the bias above, plus the luck of a concentrated book.
  3. Concentration. Ten stocks, equal-weighted at entry and allowed to drift. One NVIDIA-sized winner in a ten-stock book moves the whole result.
Real-life example — Abraham Wald's bombers

In the Second World War, analysts mapped bullet holes on bombers that came back and proposed armouring the most-hit areas. The statistician Abraham Wald pointed out that the planes hit elsewhere — the engines — never came back to be counted. Today's S&P 500 is the set of planes that came back.

6.6Breadth — why 500 stocks are only ~3.6 bets

The breadth gate converts "how many stocks" into "how many independent bets". If every pair of stocks has average return correlation ρ, then n stocks are worth

neff = n ÷ (1 + (n − 1)·ρ) → 1/ρ as n grows With ρ = 0.275 (measured): 510 stocks → neff = 3.62. Breadth BR = 3.62 × 12 rebalances = 43.4 per year. Implied IR = 0.0227 × √43.4 = 0.150 vs the bar of 0.5 → FAIL.
Real-life analogy — rowers in one boat

Five hundred passengers on one ferry are not five hundred independent journeys: if the ferry is late, everyone is late. Stocks share the market "ferry". The raw ρ = 0.275 is mostly that shared ride.

Here is the problem with the gate as built: at ρ = 0.275, BR can never exceed about 44, so passing needs |IC| ≥ 0.5 ÷ √44 ≈ 0.076 every month — roughly double the best stable ICs reported for monthly equity signals. The gate would refuse almost any realistic model. But a ranking model does not bet on the shared ride; it bets on the ε part of Part 2. The relevant ρ is the residual correlation after removing market and sector moves. The code's residual estimate (0.0014) is useless here, because it removes a market factor built from these same 510 stocks — which makes the average residual correlation ≈ −1/(n−1) by construction. This is the refinement the project deferred "until there are results" — and now there are.

PART 7

Case study II — three fits, two fixes, one honest scorecard

Part 6 dissected one model. In the following 30 hours the same model was fitted twice more, each time after fixing one thing. This part walks through what changed, what moved, and — just as important — how much of the movement we are entitled to believe.

7.2Fix 1 — sectors that are really sectors

What was wrong

Five features were meant to compare each company with its own industry — "is this net margin good for a bank?". Training supplied no sector list, so every company landed in one big group, and the "sector" z-score quietly became a whole-market z-score (§3.4). The model was told it was comparing grocers with grocers; it was comparing grocers with software houses.

Classroom analogy — grading on the wrong curve

A mark of 70% is excellent in a hard engineering exam and ordinary in an easy elective. Standardising every student's mark against the whole university instead of against their own course hides exactly the information you wanted. That is what the five features were doing.

The change

Training and the daily run both read one GICS sector map (510 of 510 names). A company with no known sector now gets an empty value, not a pooled one. Declared limitation: it is today's classification, applied to the whole ten years.

What moved — and how much

  • Average IC 0.0227 → 0.0250 (+10%); t 2.16 → 2.32. Still well short of the bar.
  • The more interesting change is the shape (Figure 7, grey → amber): 2016–18 fell from 0.052 to 0.042 while 2018–24 rose. Part of the earlier "strong start" seems to have come from the market-wide version of these features — a signal that did not travel to later years. The fixed features are steadier.
  • How sure can we be? Both fits use the same stocks and days, so most of their noise is shared and the difference is far less noisy than either standard error (≈0.011) suggests. But we did not measure how much less, so the honest summary is: consistent with a small real gain, not proof of one.

7.3Fix 2 — teach the model the question you will ask it

What was wrong

The model was fitted to predict each stock's raw 21-day return, then used only to order stocks. Those are different jobs. A raw monthly return is mostly the market's swing — the part every stock shares — and squared error is dominated by the biggest numbers in the data.

Real-life example — March 2020

In March 2020 almost every S&P 500 stock fell 20–40% in a month. Fitted on raw returns, the trees work hardest on rows like these, trying to predict the crash — something a ranking cannot use, because on the day you rank, every stock is about to crash together. Fitted on ranks, March 2020 looks like any other month: some names fell less than others, and that is the lesson.

The change

On each date, the 500 forward returns are replaced by their rank, then mapped to a normal score:

ẑi,t = Φ−1( (ranki,t − ½) / nt ) With 497 names the best stock that month gets about +3.1, the median 0, the worst about −3.1 — every month, whatever the market did. Adding 8% to every stock (a good market month) changes nothing. Evaluation still uses the raw return: the target changes what the trees learn, never how they are graded.

Why a normal score rather than a plain 0–1 percentile? Because the daily card used to multiply the model's score by each stock's volatility (§7.6). A percentile is always positive, so that multiplication would have ranked mostly by volatility. A centred score keeps "above median" and "below median" apart.

What moved — and why it is believable this time

  • Out-of-sample IC 0.0250 → 0.0360 (+44%); t 2.32 → 3.23. The significance gate passed for the first time.
  • The importances changed in the way the theory predicts. Under raw returns, vol_20d was the single most-used feature and price features held 28–32% of all importance. Under ranks, price features fall to 12% and volatility leaves the top eight; growth and cash-flow features (f_operating_income_chg_1, f_revenue_rolling_chg_4, f_operating_cash_flow_chg_1) rise. Volatile stocks have the largest raw returns in both directions, so a squared-error model leaned on volatility to predict the size of moves — not their order. Remove the size, and the model turns to what distinguishes one company from another.
  • In-sample IC jumped 0.117 → 0.220. That is not good news by itself — it mostly means the rank target is easier to fit. It does mean the gap between in-sample and out-of-sample is now wider, which matters for §8.3 (regularisation).

7.4The walk-forward: did the model learn from the future?

Fit 3 was also the first to be measured by a true walk-forward: each two-year block is predicted by a model trained only on the years before it (§5.2 explained why the purged k-fold is not that). The two estimates are close — 0.0325 against 0.0360. Training only on the past costs about a tenth of the signal, not most of it. The worry raised in §6.2, that the k-fold's good early numbers came from a model that had seen later markets, is largely answered: it was not the main story.

But the walk-forward's t is 2.70. Gated on that, fit 3 would fail. Two honest measurements, one on each side of the bar: this is precisely why "which protocol gates?" is a decision to make before looking, not after.

7.5The block that would not cooperate: 2020–22

Look again at Figure 7. In 2020–22 the k-fold IC is 0.003 and the walk-forward −0.007: no signal at all, under both protocols, in the best fit. That period held a pandemic crash, a stay-at-home boom in speculative growth stocks, a meme-stock episode and, in 2022, the sharpest rate rise in forty years. Factors that had worked — profitability, low volatility, value — took turns failing.

The project declared this risk up front: "Not regime-conditioned: one model is fitted across all market states; a result from one regime is not evidence for another." This is that sentence, measured. A model that averages 0.036 but goes blind for two years in five is a different proposition from one that earns 0.036 steadily — and the average alone cannot tell them apart.

7.6A fourth finding: grading one ranking, serving another

When a gate passes for the first time, it is worth asking: passes for what, exactly? The gates and the backtest ordered stocks by the model's score ẑ. The daily card ordered them by ẑ × volatility × √21 — a rule written for a model fitted on volatility-scaled returns, which this model never was. A calm stock with a high score could be overtaken by a volatile stock with a lower one. The PASS described a ranking the card would never have shown.

Analogy — the translated essay

A student's essay is marked in English and earns an A. The school then publishes a machine translation under the same grade. The translation may be fine — but nobody marked it.

The fix (8 Oct): the card now orders by ẑ — the ranking the gates measured — and shows ẑ × σ × √21 beside it as information only. General lesson: evaluate the exact quantity you serve. Any transformation between the evaluated output and the served output is a place where a validated model becomes an unvalidated one.

7.7How much should we believe the PASS?

Three fits on the same ten years are three draws. A crude but standard correction (Bonferroni) multiplies each p-value by the number of attempts:

Measurementtp (two-sided)× 3 attemptsReading
Fit 3, purged k-fold3.230.00120.0037Survives the correction comfortably
Fit 3, walk-forward2.700.00690.021Significant at 5%, not at the project's t ≥ 3 bar
Fit 2, purged k-fold2.320.0200.061Would not survive

Read this table with care: the t ≥ 3 bar of Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016) already allows for the whole profession's searching, so correcting again for our own three tries is conservative. The fair summary is: there is a ranking signal here, probably real, modest in size, and unreliable in some market regimes. That is a typical — and respectable — place for an equity signal to be.

7.8Why it is still refused: breadth decides now

With significance passed, the only blocking gate is breadth, and §6.6 showed why it is so hard to pass: with the raw stock-to-stock correlation (ρ = 0.275), 497 names count as only 3.6 independent bets. The arithmetic below shows how completely the verdict now depends on which correlation the gate uses:

So the model passes if the residual correlation — what is left after removing market and sector moves — is below about 0.06, and fails above it. That number has not been measured yet: the code's own residual estimate removes a market factor built from these same 510 stocks, which forces the answer to ≈ −1/(n−1). Measuring it properly, against SPY and the sector ETFs, is now the single most decision-relevant measurement in the project (§8.1).

What the case study teaches
  1. Fix the data before the model. Two of the three biggest problems found this week (stale fundamentals, fake sectors) were inputs, not algorithms.
  2. Match the target to the decision. The single largest gain came from asking the model the question it would be used for — not from more data, more trees or a cleverer algorithm.
  3. Measure two ways. The walk-forward did not overturn the k-fold, but it did show the PASS is narrow.
  4. Grade what you serve. A gate is only as good as the match between what it measured and what goes out the door.
  5. Keep the count. Three attempts, written down. The fourth will be judged knowing that.
Check yourself
  1. Fit 3's simulated return fell (37% → 27%) while its IC rose. How can both be true?
  2. Why did volatility features lose importance when the target became a rank?
  3. A colleague proposes trying five more feature sets and keeping the best. What would you ask for first?
Answers

(1) IC measures the ordering of all ~500 names; the simulation holds only the top 10, in a survivorship-biased universe where the most volatile past winners are over-represented. The raw-return model leaned on those names; the rank model leans less on them — a better ranking, a less lucky top 10. (2) Volatile stocks have the biggest raw moves in both directions, so a squared-error fit used volatility to predict the size of moves. A rank has no size. (3) That the five be written down now, that all five be reported whatever their results, and that the decision rule (which measurement, what bar) be fixed before the first one runs.

PART 8

How to improve it further — without fooling yourself

Updated after case study II. In order: fix the measurement, then the problem setup, then the model. Tuning a model on a broken ruler only produces a better-looking broken result.

The trap to avoid first

Every change you try and then keep because the IC went up is one more coin-flip in the contest of §5.3. Ten tweaks tested on the same five folds can manufacture t = 3 out of nothing. Write down what you will try before you try it, count every attempt, and report the count (Bailey & López de Prado, 2014). Three attempts have now been spent on 2016–2026 (§7.7).

8.2Next: understand 2020–22 before changing the model

  • Per-feature IC by block. For each of the 61 features, compute its own rank IC in each two-year block. The features whose sign flipped in 2020–22 are the ones the model trusted and the market did not reward. This is diagnosis, not an attempt.
  • Only then, if justified, add state variables as inputs — for example the market's own trailing volatility or the cross-sectional dispersion of returns — so one model can learn that a feature matters more in calm markets than in turbulent ones. That would revisit the project's "declared limitation" on regimes (F12 D3), so it is a decision, not a tweak.

8.3Then: the problem setup and the model

  • Regularise against the wider gap. In-sample IC 0.220 against 0.036 out of sample means the trees now memorise more. Tune a few settings with nested validation (an inner split inside each training fold): fewer leaves (7–15), much larger min_child_samples (200–1,000, so each leaf spans many dates), stronger L2, and early stopping on the inner IC. Counted as one attempt.
  • A ranking objective. LightGBM's lambdarank (Burges, 2010), grouping rows by date, optimises the ordering directly. Expect a modest change; the rank target already captured most of the gain.
  • Standardise level features per date (f_eps, f_fcf, f_size_log_assets) and make missingness explicit ("reports R&D: yes/no" — f_rd_to_revenue is still near the top of the importances while 75% empty).
  • Average several seeds or the five fold models — cheap variance reduction.
  • Prune with out-of-sample evidence — permutation importance on the test folds, not gain on the training fit.
  • Explain each pick with SHAP values (Lundberg & Lee, 2017), as the explainability seat requires.

8.4Then: the portfolio around it

  • Rank buffers — replace a held stock only when it falls out of, say, the top 30.
  • A fair benchmark rule. Compare against a simple "quality + low volatility" ranking on the same protocol. If the model cannot beat the rule, the rule ranks the card.
  • Remember the transfer coefficient — long-only, ten names loses part of the signal: IR ≈ TC × IC × √BR (Clarke, de Silva & Thorley, 2002).
A realistic order of work from here
  1. Measurements that cost no attempt: residual correlation for breadth; per-feature IC by block; start the live paper record.
  2. Two written rulings: which protocol gates significance; how breadth is measured.
  3. One pre-registered attempt: nested regularisation tuning (with level standardisation and the missingness flag bundled in, declared in advance).
  4. Then stop tuning on history. Let the paper record speak for six months before the next change.
Check yourself
  1. You try 12 hyperparameter settings and keep the best, which shows t = 3.1. Is the gate honestly passed?
  2. Why might fewer leaves and bigger leaves improve out-of-sample IC?
  3. Why is a live paper record more valuable now than a fourth backtest?
Answers

(1) Not on its face: the best of 12 noisy attempts is biased upward. Count the 12, and confirm on an untouched period. (2) With tiny signal and lots of noise, small or narrow leaves memorise noise (a few bad weeks); larger leaves force each rule to hold across many dates. (3) Every year of history has already influenced three decisions; the future has influenced none. It is the only clean test left.
